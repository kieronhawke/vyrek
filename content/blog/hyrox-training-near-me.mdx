<Callout tone="info" title="The short answer">
Almost any gym with a rower, some dumbbells and a space to run can prepare you for this race. The equipment you are worried about missing is usually the equipment that matters least. Here is how to check properly.
</Callout>

This is the question I get most from people who have just entered their first race, and it is almost always asked with more anxiety than it needs.

People look at the eight stations, see specialist kit, and assume they need to find somewhere that has all of it. Then they either pay for a second membership they do not need, or they put off entering because they think their gym is not good enough.

Neither is usually true.

## Audit what you already have

Before you look at anything new, work out what your current gym covers. Run through this at your gym rather than from memory, because most people are wrong about at least two of them.

If you ticked the first two and any two others, you can train for this race properly. Everything else is convenience.

That sled ranking surprises people, so let me defend it. The sled is a technique station more than a fitness station. What it demands is heavy leg drive with your heart rate already high, and you can build that with steep treadmill work, heavy carries and long split-squat sets. What you cannot build without a sled is the *feel* of it, and that takes one or two sessions, not twelve weeks.

Find a sled once about six weeks out, learn the body position, and you have solved it.

## When it is worth finding somewhere else

There are two situations where I would tell you to look for another gym.

**You genuinely cannot run.** No treadmill, and nowhere safe or sensible to run outside. That is a real problem, because half of this race is running and the running is the part that separates finish times. Solve this one.

**You want the race-simulation environment.** Some gyms run HYROX-format classes with the full station setup. That is genuinely useful, mostly for learning pacing and transitions under something like race conditions rather than for fitness. If there is one near you, a session every few weeks in the back half of a training block is worth it. It is a nice-to-have, not a requirement.

Beyond those two, a second membership is usually money that would be better spent on programming.

## What to actually look for locally

If you are searching, ignore the branding and check three things:

1. **Can you run from the door, or is there a treadmill you can get on when you need it?** Peak-time treadmill queues have derailed more training blocks than missing sleds have.
2. **Is there a rower you can use for twenty minutes without someone hovering?**
3. **Will they let you do slightly odd things?** Carrying dumbbells for two hundred metres, throwing a ball at a wall, dragging weight. Some gyms are relaxed about this and some are not, and it is worth finding out before you join rather than after.

That third one matters more than any equipment list. A gym with everything that will not let you use it properly is worse than a basic gym that leaves you alone.

<Callout tone="tip" title="Before you pay for anything">
Go to your current gym and do this session: 1km run, 500m row, 20 dumbbell thrusters, 200m carry, 1km run. If you can complete it without being stopped by staff or by a missing piece of kit, your gym is fine. Spend the money on a plan instead.
</Callout>
