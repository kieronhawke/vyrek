<Callout tone="info" title="The short version">
There is no single HYROX workout, which is why searching for one is frustrating. HYROX is a race format. What prepares you for it is four types of session, and if you understand what each one is for you can build your own week without a template.
</Callout>

People type "HYROX workout" into Google expecting to find a session they can do. What they usually get is either a full race simulation, which is far too much for a normal Tuesday, or a random circuit that happens to involve a sled.

Neither of those is what training for this race looks like.

## What the race actually demands

Before the sessions, the thing they are all built around. A HYROX asks for three qualities at once:

## The four sessions that matter

Everything worth doing falls into one of these four categories. A sensible week picks three of them.

### 1. Easy aerobic running

Conversational pace, thirty to sixty minutes. Boring, and the most valuable thing on this list.

This is where the engine gets built. If you can only do one type of session, do this one. Athletes who neglect easy running spend the whole race at a heart rate they cannot sustain, and no amount of sled work fixes that.

### 2. Compromised running intervals

The signature session for this race and the one that separates prepared athletes from fit ones.

The pattern: do a station, then run, then repeat. Something like a 500m row, then 800m running, four to five times through. Your legs will feel completely unlike they do fresh, and getting used to that is the entire point.

<PullQuote attribution="Benjamin Sutherland">
Your fresh 5k time is nearly irrelevant to this race. What matters is what you can run 400 metres after a sled push, and you only find that out by doing it.
</PullQuote>

### 3. Strength endurance work

Not heavy singles. Long sets, moderate loads, short rests, aimed at the stations that hurt: lunges, carries, sled-adjacent leg drive, wall balls.

Think sets of ten to twenty reps with enough load to be genuinely hard by the end, with the rest kept short enough that you never quite recover.

### 4. The simulation

The full race, or a meaningful chunk of it. Once every four to six weeks, not weekly.

This is a test, not training. It tells you your pacing, where your technique falls apart, and what your realistic finish time is. Then you go back to the other three sessions and fix whatever it exposed.

## Three sessions you can do this week

Real sessions, scaled for someone with a race in the diary and a normal gym.

**Session A, compromised running.** Five rounds of: 500m row, then 800m run. Rest ninety seconds between rounds. Keep the row honest but controlled; the run is the point, not the row.

**Session B, strength endurance.** Four rounds of: 20 walking lunges holding a dumbbell, 200m carry with something heavy in each hand, 15 wall balls or dumbbell thrusters, 250m ski erg or row. Rest two minutes between rounds.

**Session C, easy running.** Forty minutes at a pace where you could hold a conversation. If you cannot hold a conversation, you are going too fast, and going too fast makes this session worse rather than better.

Three sessions, and between them they cover every quality the race asks for.

<Callout tone="tip" title="The mistake nearly everyone makes">
Doing session B three times a week because it feels productive, and never doing session C because it feels too easy. That produces someone who is very good at circuits and who falls apart on the fifth kilometre of running. Do the easy running. It is the session that works.
</Callout>

## What about the sled?

Notice there is no sled in the three sessions above. That is deliberate.

The sled is a technique station, and the fitness it demands is built by the strength endurance work. If you have access to one, add a few pushes to session B every couple of weeks to keep the movement familiar. If you do not, our [guide to training without the kit](/blog/hyrox-training-near-me) covers the substitutes properly.

What you should not do is build your week around the one station you find most intimidating. That is a very common way to arrive at a race with a good sled push and eight kilometres of running you cannot handle.

## Putting it into a week

The simplest structure that works for three days: one easy run, one compromised running session, one strength endurance session. Spread them out. Do not stack the two hard ones back to back.

If you have four days, add a second easy run rather than a second hard session. Almost everyone's instinct is the opposite, and almost everyone's instinct is wrong here.
