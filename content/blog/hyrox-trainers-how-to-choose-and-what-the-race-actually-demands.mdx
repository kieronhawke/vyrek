**This page is about choosing trainers.** For the rest of the kit, see [what to wear](/blog/what-to-wear-for-hyrox), [what to buy](/blog/hyrox-beginner-kit-list) and [what to pack](/blog/hyrox-race-day-bag-checklist).

A HYROX shoe has to do two things that pull in opposite directions: run 8km, and hold you steady while you drive a loaded sled. Soft, high-stack cushioning helps the first and hurts the second. Every trainer marketed for this race is a compromise between those two jobs, and choosing well means knowing which side you need to favour.

## Why trust this

Two things this page will not do. It will not rank specific models, because we have not tested them and inventing a verdict would be dishonest. It will not tell you a shoe makes you faster, because the evidence for that in a mixed event like this is thin.

What it will do is set out what each of the eight stations actually asks of a shoe, from the official 26/27 rulebook's distances and loads, so you can judge any model against the real demands rather than against marketing copy.

## What the race asks of a shoe

| Element | Underfoot demand |
|---|---|
| 8 x 1km running | Cushioning and comfort. Roughly half your race time |
| [SkiErg](/hyrox/stations/ski-erg) 1000m | Almost nothing. You are standing still |
| [Sled push](/hyrox/stations/sled-push) 50m | Grip and a firm platform. Soft foam compresses and leaks force |
| [Sled pull](/hyrox/stations/sled-pull) 50m | Grip, especially on the heel, and lateral stability |
| [Burpee broad jumps](/hyrox/stations/burpee-broad-jumps) 80m | Landing stability, forefoot flexibility |
| [Rowing](/hyrox/stations/rowing) 1000m | Nothing. Feet are strapped |
| [Farmers carry](/hyrox/stations/farmers-carry) 200m | Stability under load, no roll |
| [Sandbag lunges](/hyrox/stations/sandbag-lunges) 100m | Stability, and a heel you can trust |
| [Wall balls](/hyrox/stations/wall-balls) 100 reps | A stable base to squat from |

Count that up and the picture is clearer than the marketing suggests: **one station wants cushioning, five want stability, and two do not care.** But the one that wants cushioning is roughly half your race by time.

That is the whole trade-off in a sentence.

## The two failure modes

**Too soft.** A max-cushioned road shoe with a tall stack feels wonderful for the first three kilometres. Then you get on the sled and the midsole compresses under the load, your foot rolls, and you are pushing into foam instead of into the floor. The same softness makes the lunges feel unstable at station seven when your legs are already gone.

**Too firm.** A flat, hard-soled shoe is superb on the sled and the lunges. Then you have to run 8km in it, and by the fifth kilometre your calves and feet are taking a beating that costs you more than the sled ever gave back.

Most people err towards too soft, because they buy the shoe they already run in.

## How to choose your compromise

Work out which side you should favour by being honest about where you lose time.

**Favour running comfort if:** your 5km is over 28 minutes, this is your first race, you know your feet suffer on longer runs, or your finish time is decided by whether you can keep running at all. For most first-timers this is the right answer.

**Favour stability if:** you are a competent runner (sub-24 5km), you race Pro or are heavy relative to the sled, or you know from training that the sled and lunges are where you lose time.

**A practical middle:** a firmer, lower-stack running shoe with a grippy outsole. Not a racing flat, not a max-cushion trainer. This is what most of the shoes marketed as "hybrid" or "HYROX" trainers are actually attempting.

## What to check before you buy

1. **Squeeze the midsole.** If your thumb sinks in easily, it will compress under a 152kg sled too.
2. **Check the outsole rubber, especially at the heel.** The sled pull is where people slip, and a worn or slick heel is the usual cause.
3. **Run in them first.** At least 30km before race day, including one longer run. A shoe that is fine for 5km can be brutal at 8km off eight stations.
4. **Try them on late in the day.** Feet swell, and they will be more swollen still after 6km of racing.
5. **Do not buy for race day only.** A shoe you have not trained in is a variable you have introduced on the one day you wanted fewer of them.

## Things people worry about that do not matter

**There is no footwear rule.** The rulebook's clothing section sets general requirements and a forbidden-items list; it does not mandate a shoe type. Choosing trainers is a performance decision, not a compliance one.

**You cannot change shoes mid-race.** There is no practical opportunity and Roxzone time counts towards your finish. One pair, whole race.

**Brand matters far less than fit.** A well-fitting shoe from a brand nobody talks about will beat a badly fitting one that a fast athlete wears.

## Honest limitations

We have not tested specific models and will not rank them here. When we do test properly, the page will say what was tested, over what distance, and by whom.

Shoe response is also genuinely individual. Foot shape, weight, running mechanics and injury history all move the answer, and two athletes of the same ability can want opposite shoes for good reasons.

And the honest proportion: your trainers are a small lever. If you are looking for minutes, they are in your running and your pacing, not on your feet. Buy something sensible, train in it, and stop thinking about it.
