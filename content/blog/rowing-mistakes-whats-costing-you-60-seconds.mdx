The row is the one station in HYROX where doing less can make you faster. It sits at position five, immediately after burpee broad jumps have spiked your heart rate, and it is the only place in the race where you can legally sit still and breathe.

Most people race it anyway, and pay for it across the back half.

## Why trust this

The standard below is read from the official 26/27 singles rulebook, including the rest provision that most athletes do not know exists.

## The standard, exactly

1,000 metres. The monitor is reset by the judging team before you start.

- You must **remain seated** on the rower until the full 1,000m is complete.
- You **may stop rowing at any time**, place the handle back in the holder, and rest with your feet on or off the foot plates.
- You may **not stand up, leave the seat or dismount** until the 1,000m is done and a judge has confirmed completion.

That second point is the strategic one. Resting mid-row is explicitly legal.

## The five mistakes

**1. Racing the split.** The most expensive fault, and it is a decision rather than a technique error. A 20-second-faster row bought with a redlined heart rate costs you more than 20 seconds across the farmers carry, lunges and wall balls that follow.

*The fix:* pick a controlled target split and treat the first 200m as active recovery. You should leave the rower breathing more easily than you arrived.

**2. Pulling with the arms first.** The drive sequence is legs, then hips, then arms. Arms-first is weaker, less efficient, and tires the grip you need at stations six and seven.

*The fix:* push the floor away with your legs before anything else moves. Arms finish the stroke, they do not start it.

**3. Rushing the recovery.** Coming back up the slide as fast as you went down gives you no recovery time and drives the rate up without adding power.

*The fix:* roughly one count out, two counts back. The recovery is where you breathe.

**4. Over-rating.** High stroke rate feels productive and usually means short, weak strokes. Distance per stroke matters more than strokes per minute.

*The fix:* let the rate settle lower and pull with more length and force.

**5. Not knowing the rest rule.** Athletes grind through a bad patch because they believe stopping is not allowed, or they stand up at the end before a judge confirms.

*The fix:* if you need five seconds, take five seconds with the handle in the holder. And stay seated until you are told you are done.

## The row as a tactical asset

Think of the row as the only free recovery in the race, positioned deliberately after the hardest heart-rate spike.

The athletes who use it well arrive at the farmers carry able to grip, and at the lunges with legs that still work. The athletes who race it arrive at station seven with nothing, and station seven is where races quietly end.

This is not an argument for going slowly. It is an argument for going *appropriately*, which on this station usually means slower than your ego wants.

## The faults at a glance

| Fault | Costs you | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Racing the split | More than it gains, across stations 6-8 | Treat the first 200m as recovery |
| Arms before legs | Power and grip | Legs, hips, arms, in that order |
| Rushing the recovery | The only breathing room you get | One count out, two counts back |
| Over-rating | Distance per stroke | Lower rate, longer pull |
| Not knowing you can rest | Grinding a bad patch needlessly | Handle in holder, stay seated |

## The practical part: three sessions

1. **Sequencing at low rate.** 5 x 300m at an easy pace, rate capped, focusing on legs-hips-arms and a slow recovery. Technique only.
2. **Controlled 1,000s.** 3 x 1,000m at your target race split with 2 minutes rest. Learn the pace so you can hold it under adrenaline.
3. **The real one.** 4 rounds: 20m burpee broad jumps, then 1,000m row, then 1km run. This is the actual sequence and it teaches you what row effort you can afford.

## Honest limitations

Rowing technique is genuinely coachable and a session with someone watching you is worth more than any article, including this one.

The damper setting is a personal preference rather than a rule, and chasing an "optimal" number matters far less than sequencing and pacing.
