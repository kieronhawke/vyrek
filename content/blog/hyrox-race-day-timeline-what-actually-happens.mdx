Most first-timers know how to train for a HYROX and have no idea what the day itself looks like. This is the order it happens in, and the three points where getting it wrong carries a real penalty.

## Why trust this

Registration, timing chip, warm-up and start-zone rules are read from the official 26/27 singles rulebook, which covers event day in its own section.

## The order of the day

**1. Arrive earlier than feels necessary.** Large venues put real distance between the entrance and the start area. Fifteen minutes of walking inside a building is common and nobody budgets for it.

**2. Registration.** You collect your **timing chip** and wristbands. Your assigned start time is confirmed here. If it disagrees with what you were sent, this is the moment to sort it out.

**3. Chip on the ankle.** The rulebook requires the chip on an ankle **at all times** while racing. Get it wrong and you receive a **DNS** on the leaderboard even if you cross the line. There is no retrospective fix for a time that was never recorded.

**4. Bag drop and changing rooms.** Both exist at every event. Drop what you do not need, keep what you do, and remember you will not get back to your bag before you race.

**5. Warm-up area.** A dedicated space with some equipment, usually busy. Plan a warm-up that does not depend on getting a specific machine.

**6. Start zone and tunnel.** You assemble here for your wave. Your start time should be **clearly visible on your arm** — the rulebook says so and marshals check.

**7. The wave goes.** Once it does, move forward with it. Waiting in the start tunnel once your wave has begun can cost up to a **1-minute penalty**.

## The three things that carry real consequences

| Mistake | Cost |
|---|---|
| Starting in a wave other than your assigned one, without organiser approval | **Disqualification** |
| Not wearing the timing chip on an ankle | **DNS**, even if you finish |
| Waiting in the start tunnel after your wave has gone | up to **1 minute** |

The first is the one to be careful about. It is not a matter of a marshal waving you through. If you need a different wave for any reason, that is a conversation with the organisers **in advance**.

## What to do with the gap

There is usually a long wait between arriving and racing, and most people handle it badly.

**Eat before the gap, not during it.** Decide your race-morning meal in advance and bring it. Anything you want mid-race you must carry from the start, because accepting food or drink from anyone other than the aid stations is **outside assistance** and can carry a time penalty.

**Stay warm.** Halls are large and often cold near the doors. Layers you can shed late are worth more than they sound.

**Walk the Roxzone if you can.** Identify which arch is IN and which is OUT. Going through the wrong one is a **2-minute penalty**, and it happens to people following the athlete in front.

**Do not spectate your own race.** Watching earlier waves is enjoyable and it is also an hour on your feet. Sit down.

## The practical part: a race-morning checklist

1. Photo ID and entry confirmation.
2. Timing chip on the **ankle**, checked twice.
3. Start time written on your arm.
4. Race kit and trainers you have already run in.
5. Your own breakfast, eaten at a planned time.
6. Layers for before the wave and a change of clothes for after.
7. Anything you want mid-race, carried from the start.
8. Break plans for the sled push, the lunges and the wall balls, decided in advance.

That last one is not equipment and it is the item most people skip. Arriving at a station without a plan costs more than anything you forgot to pack.

## Honest limitations

Exact schedules, wave times and hall layouts are published per event and vary considerably. This is the shape of a race day, not a timetable for yours.

Doubles and relay have their own rulebooks and their own start arrangements, so do not assume the singles detail transfers.
