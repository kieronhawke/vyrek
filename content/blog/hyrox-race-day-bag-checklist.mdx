<Callout tone="tip" title="The one rule">
Nothing in the bag should be new. Not the shoes, not the socks, not the gels, not the top. Race day is not the day to discover that something chafes at kilometre six.
</Callout>

Kit posts about this race tend to be shopping lists. This is not that. Most of what makes a race day comfortable costs nothing, and half the useful items are things people already own and forget to bring.

## What you genuinely do not need

Let me save you some money, because the kit conversation around this race gets carried away.

**Grip socks.** Nice. Not necessary. If you have not trained in them, do not debut them.

**A weight belt.** Nothing in this race requires one for the loads most people are lifting.

**Gloves.** They tend to make grip worse on the sled pull, not better, and grip is the thing they are supposed to help.

**Compression everything.** Wear it if you like how it feels. It is not going to change your finish time.

**New shoes bought for the race.** The most expensive mistake available. Shoes need miles in them before they are trustworthy, and a race is a bad place to find out where a new pair rubs.

<PullQuote attribution="Benjamin Sutherland">
Do your first race in what you own. You will finish knowing exactly which one thing actually bothered you, and then you can spend money solving that specific problem instead of guessing at five of them.
</PullQuote>

## Timing on the day

Ninety minutes before your wave is a comfortable arrival. That sounds excessive and it is not, once you have accounted for parking, registration, finding the bag drop, and the fact that everything takes longer than you think with several thousand people doing it at once.

Use the spare time properly: **go and watch a wave start**. Fifteen minutes of watching how the first station works, where people go, and how the RoxZone is laid out will do more for your nerves than any amount of warming up. First-timers who have seen it happen once are noticeably calmer.

Then warm up. Properly, not a token jog. You want to be genuinely warm before the first kilometre, because the first kilometre is the one that decides your race and going into it cold means going into it either too slow or too hard.

<Callout tone="warn" title="The night before">
Lay everything out. Not in the bag, out on the floor where you can see it. It takes five minutes and it is the difference between a calm morning and driving back for your shoes. Pin your number the night before too, if your event issues them in advance.
</Callout>

## Food, briefly

Eat what you normally eat before training. Race day is not the moment for a new breakfast, a new gel, or a large coffee if you do not usually have one.

Two to three hours before is the standard window and it is standard because it works for most people. If you have a routine that works in training, keep it. If you do not have one, now is the time to test one, in training, not on the day.

## After you finish

You will be wet, quite cold within ten minutes, and hungrier than expected shortly after that. The dry clothes, the plastic bag and the snack are the three items that turn the hour after a race from grim into pleasant.

If you have a long journey home, that hour matters more than you would think.
