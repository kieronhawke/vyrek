<Callout tone="info" title="Before we start: where these numbers come from">
These are coaching benchmarks, not a dataset. They come from racing in the Elite 15 and from coaching athletes through first races and personal bests. When I quote a range, I am telling you what I see, not publishing a statistic. Official results are the place to go for hard numbers.
</Callout>

I get asked this more than any other question, usually about three weeks out from someone's first race, and usually by someone who has just watched a video of an elite athlete finishing in under an hour and quietly panicked.

So let me answer it properly.

There is no such thing as a good HYROX time in the abstract. There is a good time for your first race off twelve weeks of training. There is a good time for someone in their third season chasing a personal best. Those are different questions with different answers, and comparing yourself to the wrong one is the fastest way to feel bad about a race you should be proud of.

If you take one thing from that chart, take this: the gap between a first-timer and a strong club athlete is about twenty minutes, and almost none of it is fitness you do not already have. It is pacing, transitions and knowing how to break the wall balls. That is learnable in a season.

The gap between a strong club athlete and the elite field is a different animal entirely. That one is genetics, years and a life arranged around training.

## Why the first kilometre decides your finish time

The single most common way people lose ten minutes is by finding the first kilometre easy.

You are fresh, the crowd is loud, you have tapered, and one kilometre feels like nothing. So you run it forty seconds quicker than you have ever run it in training, arrive at the ski erg with your heart rate already at threshold, and spend the next seven runs paying for it.

<PullQuote attribution="Benjamin Sutherland">
The first run should feel almost embarrassingly controlled. If you finish the first kilometre thinking "I could have gone faster", you have paced it correctly.
</PullQuote>

I have done this myself. Berlin, my first race, 2024. I went through the first kilometre far too quickly because it felt easy, and I spent the back half of that race negotiating with myself. Everyone does it once. The trick is only doing it once.

## A word on comparing yourself to the elite field

I race in the Elite 15. I want to be honest about what that means, because I think the comparison does more harm than good.

The people at the front of this race have organised their lives around it. They are not doing a session before work and another after the kids are in bed. Comparing your first race to their finish time is like comparing your Sunday five-a-side to the Premier League. It is the same sport and it is not the same activity.

What the elite field is genuinely useful for is technique. Watch how they hold the sled. Watch their stride length on the push. Watch how they break the wall balls into sets and how little time they spend standing still. That is all transferable. The finish time is not.

## So what should you aim for?

If it is your first race: aim to finish, and aim to have a plan for the first kilometre and the wall balls. That is it. Everything else is noise.

If it is your second: you already know where you lost time. Fix the biggest one. Most people find three to five minutes in transitions and pacing alone, without getting any fitter.

If you are chasing a specific number: you need a plan built around your weakest station, not your strongest. Everyone wants to train the thing they are good at. The time is in the thing they avoid.

<Callout tone="tip" title="Where most people find their next five minutes">
Not in the gym. In the RoxZone, in the first kilometre, and in the wall balls. Those three between them account for more lost time than every strength deficit combined, and none of them require you to get fitter.
</Callout>
