A deload week is a planned drop in training volume that keeps some intensity, taken **before** you need it rather than after.

It is not a rest week. It is not a taper. Confusing it with either is why most people either skip it or do it wrong.

## Why trust this

A twelve-week HYROX build asks for running and loaded station work in the same weeks, per the format itself: 8km of running in eight pieces plus eight stations, under the official 26/27 rulebook. That is a lot of concurrent load, and concurrent load is precisely the case where absorbed training matters more than accumulated training.

## Deload, rest week, taper: they are three different things

| | What changes | Intensity | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Deload** | Volume drops by roughly a third to a half for one week | Kept, in small doses | Every third or fourth week, mid-block |
| **Rest week** | Almost everything stops | None | After a race, or when something is wrong |
| **Taper** | Volume drops further and does not return | Kept, sharp, brief | The final week before a race |

The distinction that matters: a deload is part of the build. You come out of it and go straight back up. A taper is the end of the build and there is nothing after it but the race.

## When to take one

**The calendar answer:** every fourth week in a twelve-week block. That gives you week 4 and week 8 as deloads, with week 12 as the taper.

**The better answer:** when the signals say so, which is often week three for people over 40, people sleeping badly, or anyone whose life outside training got heavier.

The signals worth watching, in rough order of usefulness:

1. **Resting heart rate elevated two mornings running.** The most reliable of the lot, and free if you already wear a watch.
2. **The same session feeling harder at the same pace or load,** across more than one session.
3. **Sleep getting worse, not better, in a hard week.** Counter-intuitive and very common.
4. **Irritability and flatness outside training.** People notice this in you before you notice it in yourself.
5. **A niggle that has stopped settling overnight.**

Two of those together is a deload. Do not wait for four.

## What a deload week actually looks like

Cut volume by a third to a half. Keep the shape of the week and keep a small amount of the hard stuff.

| Normal week | Deload week |
|---|---|
| 3 runs, one of them long | 2 runs, both shorter, one with a few short efforts |
| 2 strength sessions | 1 strength session, same movements, fewer sets |
| 1 HYROX-specific session | Skip it, or halve it |
| Long easy run at the weekend | Half the distance, easy |

What you are not doing is turning every session into a slow jog. Intensity is what preserves sharpness, and it is cheap to keep: two or three short efforts inside an easy run costs you almost nothing to recover from and stops the week feeling like starting again.

## The practical part

1. **Put the deload in the calendar at the start of the block,** not when you feel you need it. The week you feel you need it is a week late.
2. **Halve the volume, keep the pattern.** Same days, same session types, less of each.
3. **Keep one hard touch.** A handful of short efforts, or one heavy set of a lift you know well.
4. **Do not add anything.** A deload is not an opportunity to try a new class or a long walk you would not normally do.
5. **Sleep is the actual session.** If you do one thing differently, go to bed earlier for seven nights.
6. **Come back at the volume you left, not above it.** The temptation after a good deload is to make up for it. That is how the next block starts already dug in.

## Honest limitations

Every number here is a starting structure, not a prescription. A third to a half is the range that works for most people, and some need more.

This also assumes you are training enough for a deload to mean something. If you are training three times a week and feeling fine, you may not need a scheduled one at all, and the fatigue you are feeling might be sleep, work or under-eating rather than training load.

And a deload cannot fix an injury. It reduces load, which helps, but a problem that needs assessment still needs assessment.
