Home personal training is a coach coming to you with portable equipment. It removes the travel, the gym intimidation and the childcare problem in one go, which is why it suits some people enormously and why it is oversold to everyone else.

The honest limit is load. A coach can carry kettlebells; they cannot carry a squat rack.

## What actually happens

The coach arrives with portable kit, usually kettlebells, bands, a mat, sometimes suspension straps or a sandbag. You clear a space. The session runs like any other, then they pack up and leave.

Sessions are typically the same length as gym-based ones. Some coaches charge a travel premium and some build it into their rate.

## What you can genuinely train at home

**Conditioning.** Circuits, intervals, bodyweight work. Barely compromised.

**Kettlebell strength and power.** Swings, cleans, presses, squats, carries. A well-programmed kettlebell block builds real strength for most people.

**Mobility and movement quality.** Arguably better at home, where you are more likely to repeat it.

**Single-leg and unilateral work.** Split squats, step-ups, lunges. All fine with modest load.

**Anything for a beginner.** For the first several months, portable equipment is not the constraint. Your capacity is.

## What genuinely needs a gym

**Progressive heavy barbell work.** If your goal is a heavier squat or deadlift, there is a ceiling on what fits in a car.

**Sled work.** No sled in a living room.

**Machine-based rehabilitation.** Some prescribed rehab needs specific equipment.

**Very heavy loading generally.** At some point the kettlebells run out, and that point arrives sooner than enthusiastic marketing suggests.

For HYROX specifically, home training covers the running, the burpees, the lunges and the wall ball pattern. It does not cover the sleds or the ergs, which is a real gap.

| Goal | Home training | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| General fitness / fat loss | Kettlebells, bands, bodyweight | Barely compromised |
| Conditioning and intervals | Needs almost nothing | Fine |
| Beginner strength | Kettlebells go a long way | Fine for months |
| Heavy barbell progression | Cannot carry a rack | Needs a gym |
| Sled work | No | Needs a gym |
| Machine-based rehab | Depends on prescription | Usually needs a gym |
| Mobility | Better at home, honestly | Fine |

## The practical questions nobody asks

1. **What space do you actually need?** Ask before booking. Most coaches need less than you think, but "less than you think" is not "none".
2. **What do you need me to have?** Some coaches expect a mat or a step. Establish it.
3. **Does the rate include travel?** And is there a distance limit.
4. **What happens to the floor?** Kettlebells and laminate are a bad combination. Ask what they put down.
5. **Who else will be in the house?** Not a trivial question. A session with a toddler present is a different session, and a good coach will plan for it rather than be surprised.
6. **What is the cancellation policy?** Home sessions get cancelled more often than gym ones, in both directions.

## Who it suits

**Parents of young children**, where the travel and childcare are the entire barrier.

**People who find gyms genuinely unpleasant**, and there are more of them than the industry admits.

**Anyone with a punishing schedule** where twenty minutes of travel each way is what kills consistency.

**Returners after a long break** who want to rebuild in private first.

## Who it does not suit

**Anyone chasing a heavy strength goal.**

**People whose home is chaotic.** If you cannot reliably clear a space and be uninterrupted, you have moved the problem rather than solved it.

**Anyone who needs the gym to feel like training.** Some people cannot switch modes at home. That is a real thing and worth knowing about yourself.

## The pattern that works well

A block of home sessions to build the habit and learn the movements, then a move to a gym once load becomes the limiter. Six to twelve weeks is a common shape.

Going the other way also works: gym sessions when you have time, home sessions during a busy period, rather than stopping entirely.

## Honest limitations

Home training's ceiling is real but it is much higher than most people reach. If you are not yet at the point where a kettlebell is too light, the ceiling is not your problem.

Coach availability is also more limited for home sessions, and travel time means fewer slots.
