<Callout tone="warn" title="Check the current rulebook">
This page explains the standards in plain English and tells you which ones actually catch people out. It is not a substitute for the official rulebook, which is the authority and can be updated between seasons. Read it once before your first race.
</Callout>

Almost nobody fails a HYROX on the rules. What happens instead is quieter and more expensive: people lose thirty seconds here and a minute there getting reps sent back, usually at the end, usually when they are too tired to be precise.

So this is not a lawyer's guide to the rulebook. It is the list of things I have watched cost people time, and what to do about them.

## Station by station, what the judge is watching

**Ski erg and rower.** The simplest stations to judge. Complete the distance shown on the monitor. The only thing to be careful about is starting the machine properly and not sitting down before the number is reached.

**Sled push and sled pull.** The sled must fully cross the line at each end. Judges are watching that you complete the full distance, not how you look doing it. There is no style mark on the sled.

**Burpee broad jump.** Chest to the floor at the bottom, and each jump must cover the minimum distance. Two feet take off and land together. Stepping through is not a broad jump.

**Farmers carry.** Carry the full distance. You may set it down and rest, and you restart from that point. What you cannot do is move forward while it is on the floor.

**Sandbag lunges.** The one worth practising to standard. Trailing knee touches the floor on every rep, and the bag stays where it is meant to be on your back or shoulders. This is the station where fatigue quietly turns lunges into something that is not quite a lunge.

**Wall balls.** The station that decides races and the station with the most reps to get wrong. Hip crease below the top of the knee at the bottom, ball hits the target at the top. Both, every rep, one hundred times.

<PullQuote attribution="Benjamin Sutherland">
Nobody no-reps their first wall ball. The problem is rep seventy-eight, when your legs have gone and your squat gets four inches shallower without you noticing.
</PullQuote>

## How to train the standards out of the problem

The fix is not learning the rules. Everyone reading this now knows the rules. The fix is being able to hold the standard when you are wrecked, and that is a training problem.

## Penalties, and what actually happens

The word penalty makes people nervous, so let me deflate it.

In the vast majority of cases, a fault is called at the time and you repeat the rep. The judge tells you, you do it again, you carry on. It is not dramatic, nobody is annoyed with you, and it happens to almost everyone at some point.

The more serious outcomes exist and are genuinely rare. They involve things like missing a station, cutting the running course, taking outside assistance, or ignoring a judge. If you are running the race honestly, none of these will happen to you by accident.

The one thing worth knowing: **do what the judge says, immediately, without arguing.** They are managing a lot of athletes and they are not out to get you. Arguing costs more time than the rep would have.

<Callout tone="tip" title="The most useful rule to know on the day">
You may rest at any station. Setting the sandbag down, letting go of the farmers handles, standing over the wall ball for five seconds. None of that breaks a rule. Athletes lose more time refusing to rest and then collapsing into a two-minute stop than they would have lost taking three ten-second breaks.
</Callout>

## Before your first race

Read the official rulebook once. It takes twenty minutes and it removes the low-level anxiety of not quite knowing what is allowed, which is worth more than the rules themselves.

Then practise the two standards that actually get enforced under fatigue: wall ball depth and the lunge knee touch. Everything else on this page will look after itself.
