<Callout tone="info" title="Who this is for">
Someone who does not currently train, cannot run a kilometre without stopping, and has entered or is thinking about entering a HYROX. If that is you, this is a realistic route. It is not a fast one, and that is the point.

Already able to walk briskly for half an hour? You do not need all twenty-four weeks. The [16-week plan](/blog/hyrox-couch-to-finish) puts a four-week pre-block in front of the standard build and gets you there sooner.
</Callout>

The honest thing to say first: six months is a long time, and most plans you find will promise you twelve weeks.

Twelve weeks works if you already train. From genuinely nothing, twelve weeks is how people end up with shin splints in week seven, or arrive at a start line dreading the next two hours. Twenty-four weeks is not me being cautious. It is the runway your connective tissue actually needs.

## Phase 1, weeks 1 to 6: show up

**What you actually do:** Three sessions a week. Two are walk-run: five minutes walking, then alternate one minute of gentle running with two minutes walking, for twenty minutes total. One is a simple full-body strength session, six or seven movements, nothing heavy.

**What it feels like:** Easier than you expected physically, harder than you expected to fit in. The battle in this phase is entirely logistical.

**How you know it is working:** You did three sessions in a week, three weeks running. That is the whole test. Do not measure anything else yet.

<PullQuote attribution="Benjamin Sutherland">
The first six weeks are not about fitness. They are about becoming a person who trains on a Tuesday. Everything else is built on that and nothing works without it.
</PullQuote>

## Phase 2, weeks 7 to 12: build the engine

**What you actually do:** The running intervals grow. By week twelve you are aiming to run twenty to twenty-five minutes continuously at an easy pace. Strength sessions add lunges, carries and squatting patterns, which is to say the race movements, without calling them that yet.

**What it feels like:** This is where it starts feeling like training. It is also where most people quit, usually around week nine, when the novelty has gone and the race is still far away.

**How you know it is working:** You can run continuously for twenty minutes without needing to stop. Not fast. Continuously.

## Phase 3, weeks 13 to 18: learn the race

**What you actually do:** Compromised running arrives, which means doing a station and then running on tired legs. Station work becomes specific: wall balls, lunges with load, carries, ergs. Around week seventeen or eighteen, a partial simulation, perhaps four runs and four stations.

**What it feels like:** Genuinely hard, and genuinely interesting. This is the phase where people start enjoying it, because it stops being generic fitness and starts being preparation for a specific thing.

**How you know it is working:** You can run 800 metres straight after a station without walking. That is the race-specific skill and this is where it is built.

## Phase 4, weeks 19 to 24: sharpen and taper

**What you actually do:** A full simulation around week twenty or twenty-one. Then several weeks using what it told you: refining pacing, tidying whatever technique fell apart, deciding how you will break the wall balls. The last ten days, volume drops substantially.

**What it feels like:** The taper feels wrong. Everyone thinks they are losing fitness. Nobody is.

**How you know it is working:** You know your target pace for the first kilometre and you know your wall ball plan. Those two decisions matter more on race day than anything else you have done.

## The three things that derail people

**Going too fast in phase 1.** The running intervals are supposed to feel almost embarrassingly gentle. If you are out of breath in week two, you are running too hard and you are building toward an injury rather than a race.

**Adding a fourth session in phase 2.** It feels like progress. It is usually the thing that produces the niggle that costs you three weeks.

**Skipping the easy running for the exciting station work.** Wall balls are more fun than a thirty-minute jog. The thirty-minute jog is what gets you round.

## What finishing actually looks like

I want to set expectations properly, because the videos you have seen are not representative.

Off twenty-four weeks from nothing, most people finish somewhere around two hours, give or take. Some of the runs will be walked. The wall balls will be broken into small sets and they will take a long time. You will feel, in the middle of it, that this was a stupid idea.

And then you finish, and it will be one of the better things you have done. I have watched a lot of people cross that line and the reaction is remarkably consistent.

[What counts as a good time](/blog/what-is-a-good-hyrox-time) has the fuller picture, but for a first race off this plan: finishing is the benchmark. There is no other one.
