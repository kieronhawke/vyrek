Somebody asked me last month why their first race had cost them nearly four hundred pounds when the entry had been a fraction of that.

The answer was a hotel in a city centre on a race weekend, booked eleven days out, plus two train tickets bought the same week. None of that is the race's fault. All of it was avoidable.

So this is the post I wish existed when I did my first one: what a race weekend actually costs, where the money goes, and which parts you can cut without making the experience worse.

## Where to actually save money

Four things, in the order they will save you the most.

**Book accommodation the same day you book your entry.** This is the single biggest saving available and it costs you nothing but ten extra minutes. Room rates near a venue on a race weekend go one direction. If you are on the fence about which race to enter, the accommodation cost should be part of that decision, not an afterthought.

**Pick a race you can reach on the morning.** A race two hours away that you can drive to at six in the morning is dramatically cheaper than one three hundred miles away, and for a first race the local one is usually the better experience anyway: familiar roads, own bed, no travel fatigue in your legs.

**Travel with people.** Sharing a car and a room roughly halves everything except entry. It also makes the weekend considerably better, and if you are racing doubles you are travelling with your partner regardless.

**Do not buy anything.** This is the one people ignore, so let me be blunt about it.

<Callout tone="tip" title="On buying kit for a first race">
You do not need new shoes. You do not need grip socks, a belt, gloves or a branded bag. Do your first race in whatever you have trained in. You will finish it knowing exactly which one thing genuinely bothered you, and then you can spend money solving that specific problem instead of guessing at five of them.
</Callout>

## Is it worth it?

I think so, but I want to be straight about why, because the reason matters.

The race itself is a good day. It is well run, the atmosphere is genuinely excellent, and finishing one is a real thing to have done. But that is not where the value is.

The value is in the twelve weeks before it. A dated event in the calendar changes how you train. It turns "I should do some conditioning" into "I have eight weeks and my wall balls are terrible". Most people I coach get more out of the preparation than they do out of the race, and they only did the preparation because they had entered something.

<PullQuote attribution="Benjamin Sutherland">
You are not really buying a race. You are buying a deadline. The deadline is the thing that works.
</PullQuote>

So the honest test is this: if you would train exactly the same way without an entry in the diary, then you are paying for a T-shirt and a finish-line photo, and there is nothing wrong with that as long as you know it. If having a date in the calendar would change what you do on a wet Tuesday in November, the entry fee is the best money you will spend on your fitness this year.

## Budget your training as well

One thing people forget: the race is one day, and the training is three months.

If you already have a gym you can run and lift in, that cost is already in your life and the race adds nothing to it. If you are joining somewhere specifically for this, add three months of membership to the figure the calculator gave you, because that is part of the true cost.
